The Pursuer-Withdrawer Dynamic: Why One Partner Chases and the Other Runs Away

What happens when one person seeks connection and the other needs distance?

You sense that something has changed between you and your partner. They seem more distant than usual, so you ask if something is wrong. “Nothing,” they tell you. But you can feel the distance, and their reassurance does little to settle you.

So you ask again. Perhaps you begin looking for other signs that something is wrong. You want to talk about the relationship, understand what is happening, and feel reassured that the connection is still secure. Your partner, meanwhile, experiences your questions as pressure. The more you try to talk, the quieter they become. Eventually, they ask for space, or disappear and stop responding altogether.

Their distance confirms exactly what you were afraid of: Something is wrong. So you try harder to reach them, but they seemingly retreat further.

If you've ever found yourself repeatedly in some version of this scenario, you may be familiar with a common and often detrimental relationship pattern: the pursuer-withdrawer dynamic.

One partner seeks greater connection, communication, reassurance, or resolution. The other, feeling overwhelmed, criticized, controlled, or unable to respond adequately, creates distance. They may become quiet, change the subject, emotionally shut down, leave the conversation, or ask for space.

Both reactions are typically driven by fear, rather than an intention to harm the other person or the connection. Nonetheless, each person's attempt to make the situation feel safer inadvertently makes the other person feel less safe, creating a vicious cycle that can be difficult to break.

The more the withdrawer distances, the more anxious the pursuer becomes. The pursuer may ask more questions, send more messages, become increasingly emotional, criticize, or insist that the problem be resolved immediately. The more intensely they pursue, however, the more overwhelmed the withdrawer becomes, and the further they retreat.

Eventually, both people become convinced that the other person is the problem.

Underneath the Cycle

What makes this dynamic particularly difficult is that the visible behavior often obscures what is happening underneath it.

The pursuing partner may appear angry, demanding, or critical, while underneath they may be frightened: Do you care about me? Are you going to leave? Does this relationship matter to you?

The withdrawing partner may appear indifferent, dismissive, or uncaring, while internally they may be experiencing something quite different: I can't get this right. Nothing I say will make this better. I need to get away from this before it becomes worse.

Attachment patterns can help us understand why some people are vulnerable to their own role in this cycle. Those with more anxious attachment tendencies may respond to perceived disconnection by intensifying their efforts to restore closeness. Those with more avoidant tendencies may manage relational distress by creating distance, suppressing emotion, or emphasizing autonomy.

Neither strategy is inherently malicious. Often, both developed as ways of protecting ourselves in relationships long before our current partner entered the picture. But what once protected us can eventually interfere with our ability to connect and, if left unaddressed, can end important relationships.

Creating Awareness

One of the most important shifts couples can make is moving away from asking, “Which one of us is causing this?” toward asking, “What happens between us when we both feel threatened?”

The dynamic becomes easier to understand when we stop looking at each person's behavior in isolation. Both partners contribute something to the maintenance of the pattern. Pursuit reinforces withdrawal, and withdrawal reinforces pursuit.

This also means that repairing the dynamic requires both people to take responsibility for their part.

For the pursuer, this might mean learning to tolerate some uncertainty, communicate vulnerability rather than criticism, regulate emotions before seeking resolution, and allow another person space without immediately interpreting distance as abandonment.

For the withdrawer, it might mean learning to remain emotionally present when discomfort arises, communicate the need for space without disappearing, initiate difficult conversations rather than perpetually avoiding them, and recognize that disengagement affects the other person, too.

Taking responsibility for your part does not mean taking responsibility for someone else's behavior, nor does understanding another person's attachment patterns require tolerating chronic neglect, hostility, manipulation, or emotional unavailability. Understanding a pattern simply gives us greater freedom to decide whether, and how, we want to participate in it.

Beyond Romantic Relationships

Although we most commonly pay attention to this cycle in the context of couples, these patterns rarely exist only in romantic relationships. They may show up in our relationships with family, in friendships, or even in our professional relationships.

Someone who pursues a partner for reassurance may also repeatedly seek feedback from a supervisor when they feel uncertain. Someone who withdraws during romantic conflict may avoid difficult conversations with colleagues, disappear when overwhelmed, or emotionally disengage when expectations feel impossible to meet.

This is why self-awareness matters so profoundly. We cannot control how another person behaves toward us, nor can we control whether they are willing to examine what they bring to the challenges within a relationship. But we can become increasingly aware of what happens inside us when something within ourselves or the connection feels threatened, and slowly learn to respond differently rather than simply repeating old patterns.

How Therapy Can Help

Individual therapy can provide a space to understand where our relational patterns originated, recognize the situations that activate them, and explore the fears and vulnerabilities underneath our reactions. Over time, this awareness can help us develop a greater capacity to manage our own triggers and choose how we respond, rather than automatically reverting to familiar ways of protecting ourselves.

When both partners are willing to examine their contribution to the dynamic, couples therapy can help make the cycle itself visible. Rather than positioning one partner as the problem, the work can focus on understanding what each person experiences, how one person's protective response activates the other's, and how both partners can begin creating a different interaction together.

Sometimes, changing a relationship begins not with getting the other person to behave differently. It may start with us recognizing the role we have unknowingly been playing all along, and becoming willing to do something different.
