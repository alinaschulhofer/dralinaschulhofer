Why Getting What You Want Can Feel Unsafe

How the inner parts of us trying to protect us can hold us back from the success, love, and connection we want.

By Dr. Alina Schulhofer

While you may consciously want something, a part of you can still feel unsafe when it starts to become possible.

For different people, this can show up in different ways. Consciously, you want the relationship but repeatedly find yourself pulling away or starting a fight when intimacy increases. You may wish for greater visibility professionally but keep postponing the tasks that would put your work into the world. You’ve worked hard for an opportunity, yet when it arrives, you begin questioning whether you belong there at all.

These patterns are often described as imposter syndrome or self-sabotage, but those labels don’t always help us understand what’s happening beneath the surface.

In my work, I find it helpful to think about the different inner parts of us that show up in these moments. While one part may feel ambitious, capable, and ready, another may carry memories of being criticized, rejected, or made to feel unworthy. These parts can have different ideas about which relationships, opportunities, or levels of success feel safe and compatible with how we see ourselves.

While you may consciously want success, another part of you may associate being successful with being envied, isolated, or the possibility of eventual failure or loss. A part of you may crave a lasting, intimate relationship, but another may have learned that closeness comes with disappointment or abandonment.

Sometimes, people are aware of patterns of self-sabotage or notice that relationships or opportunities keep falling apart at the eleventh hour. In these moments, instead of shifting to self-blame or asking, “Why do I keep messing things up?” it can be more useful to examine: “What does this part of me fear would happen if I moved forward?”

Perhaps being more seen professionally also means risking greater criticism. Perhaps greater success would come with higher expectations that a part of you is scared you can’t sustain. Stepping into a different identity may feel like moving away from the people, relationships, or familiar roles that have historically provided a sense of belonging.

What looks like resistance or self-sabotage may be your inner world’s attempt to protect you, even if that protection also limits your happiness and success.

In my experience working with high performers, there is often a striking gap between what someone has already achieved and what they privately believe about themselves. From the outside, they may appear confident and accomplished; internally, they may still feel inadequate or undeserving.

That inner conflict can prevent them from reaching a new level and often becomes especially pronounced when the stakes rise: an important negotiation, a decisive game, an opportunity they have spent years preparing for.

In therapy or coaching, we can begin to recognize our inner voices and better understand the unconscious experiences and beliefs associated with them. The goal is to understand the different parts within us, the experiences they carry, and the functions they serve. As we learn to work with these parts, we can create more room for the relationships, opportunities, and experiences we consciously want.

We can strengthen our connection to the parts of us that recognize our worth and develop a more integrated identity, where all parts have a chance to be heard and acknowledged, without allowing the inner voice that is fearful or feels inadequate to dictate our choices and what feels possible for us.
